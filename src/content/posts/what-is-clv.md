Closing line value (CLV) is a simple comparison: **the number you bet versus the number the market closed at.** It is not a story about who covered. It is not a trophy for last night. It is a grade on the *ticket price*.

If you only remember one sentence from this lesson, make it this: **a win can still be a bad price, and a loss can still be a good one.**

## The hook, without the folklore

Sports betting markets spend hours (sometimes days) digesting information: injuries, weather, pitching changes, public money, and whatever else can move a quote. By first pitch or kickoff, that quote has usually been tested by more people, with more limits, than the number you saw at breakfast.

The **closing line** is that last widely available price. CLV asks: relative to that last price, did you buy cheap or expensive?

You can cash a bet at a worse number than the close. You can lose a bet at a better number than the close. The result of one game is mostly noise. The gap between your price and the close is a cleaner — still imperfect — look at whether your process is finding a number the market later agrees was too generous.

## Core idea: beat the close, not the narrative

A process with an edge is trying, over a large sample, to be on the right side of the *price*. That shows up as **consistently beating the close** (positive CLV). One Sunday does not prove it. One bad bounce does not disprove it.

Think of two scoreboards:

| Scoreboard | What it measures | What it is good for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tonight’s result | Whether this ticket cashed | Entertainment, bankroll movement, very little process info |
| CLV | Whether you paid a better number than the close | A running grade on price discipline |

If you only watch the first board, you will overfit stories. If you watch the second, you can still be wrong — closes are not oracles — but you are at least grading the thing you control: **the number you took.**

## A plain example

Suppose you bet a side at **−110**. By close, that same side is **−120**.

You got a better price than the market’s last quote. That is **positive CLV**. The extra juice you did *not* pay is the whole point of the comparison.

Reverse it. You bet **−110**. The close is **−105** (or even money). The market got cheaper after you bought. That is **negative CLV**. You may still cash. The ticket was still expensive relative to the close.

You do not need a spreadsheet to see the direction:

- **Your number is better than the close → positive CLV**
- **Your number is worse than the close → negative CLV**

People argue about *how* to compute the edge in cents or percentage points. Fine. Get the direction right first. Do not let a cashed ticket talk you out of a bad price, and do not let a loss talk you out of a good one.

## Why this matters (and what it is not)

CLV is a **price-discipline scoreboard**. It answers: “Did I transact at a number the later market respected?” It does not answer: “Am I due?” It does not answer: “Is this team good?” It does not make a lock.

It also is not magic:

- **The close can be wrong.** Thin books, late news, and limited markets produce sloppy closes.
- **Not every close is the same close.** Shop. A −119 at one book and −110 at another are different quotes.
- **Beating a bad close is not a personality.** If you only beat numbers nobody is willing to hold, you may be measuring a ghost.

Used honestly, CLV keeps you from confusing a lucky weekend with a method. Used as a slogan (“I beat the close so I’m a genius”), it is just another narrative.

## What to do with it as a reader

Sporty Media will talk about CLV because it is how you keep score on *education*: did you understand the number, or did you understand the story? We will not tell you to bet a side so you can “collect CLV.” That sentence is a pick in a lab coat.

If you keep a record, record the **price you actually got** and the **close at the book you care about**. Grade the price. Let the game result sit in a different column.

## Close

CLV grades the ticket price, not the narrative. Beat the close consistently and you have a process claim. Cash one winner and you have a story. Those are not the same thing.
